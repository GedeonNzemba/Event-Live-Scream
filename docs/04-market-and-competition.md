# 04 — Market and competition

## Sizing, honestly

Bottom-up, and with the uncomfortable conclusion stated first.

### Beachhead — Congo-Brazzaville ↔ France & Belgium

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Congo-Brazzaville-origin adults in France & Belgium | ~55,000 |
| Share with close family at home and means to pay | ~35% → 19,000 |
| Payer accounts (households, not individuals) | ~14,000 |
| Annual revenue per account | €90 |
| **Beachhead market** | **≈ €1.3M / year** |

**That is too small to build a venture-scale company on, and pretending otherwise would be
dishonest.** The beachhead is not the prize. It is the cheapest place on earth to prove
whether the model works, because the founder has native distribution into it. Prove it
there, then move.

### Serviceable market — francophone Africa ↔ Europe & North America

France had 3.7 million African-born immigrants in 2024, of whom roughly 1.5 million are from
sub-Saharan Africa; counting descendants gives something closer to 3 million people of
sub-Saharan African origin.[^insee] Belgium, Switzerland and francophone Canada add
meaningfully — Belgium's Congolese community in particular is large and long-established.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Francophone sub-Saharan-origin adults in FR/BE/CH/CA | ~1.75M |
| Addressable (close family ties, smartphone, disposable income) | ~20% → 350,000 |
| Annual revenue per account | €90 |
| **SAM** | **≈ €31M / year** |

This is a real business, and every country in it runs on a currency pegged to the euro —
see the CFA franc discussion in [document 03](./03-business-model.md).

### Total market — African diaspora globally

Roughly 15 million adults in OECD countries with close family in Africa. At 15% addressable
and €90 a year, the *streaming product alone* is on the order of **€200M/year**.

The adjacent number is larger and matters more. Remittances to sub-Saharan Africa were
**$54 billion in 2023** and have grown since.[^wb] The contribution rail described in
[document 03](./03-business-model.md) does not need much of that: 2% of the corridor at a 3%
take is $32M a year in revenue, from customers acquired for free by the streaming product.

**The strategic shape, then: a modest, high-margin, defensible services business that
acquires a very large payments business for nothing.** That is the pitch, and it should be
made in that order — never the reverse.

## The competitive landscape

### Who is actually in this space

**Professional event streamers in anglophone Africa.** Real, working businesses. Atesh
Graphics in Nakuru streams Kenyan weddings and burials to viewers in the US, Germany, the
UK, Saudi Arabia and Dubai.[^atesh] Mediaworks in Lagos has streamed events simultaneously
to ten cities across three countries.[^lagos] South Africa has a dense cluster — RICH
Productions, YouView, 3-Axis Studios — and Ghana has several outfits built specifically
around funeral streaming for the diaspora.[^ghana]

This is the most important entry in the table, because **it proves the demand is real and
people already pay for it.** It also shows the shape of the gap:

| What they do | What they don't do |
| --- | --- |
| Weddings and large funerals | Birthdays, baptisms, a Tuesday call to grandmother |
| Professional multi-camera crews | Anything at a €15–39 price point |
| Booked locally, paid locally in cash | Booked from Paris on a card, split among nine relatives |
| Anglophone Africa | **Francophone Central Africa — essentially nobody** |
| Assume the crew brings gear and power | Solve the household's power, device or labour problem |
| Best-effort live; if it drops, it drops | Guarantee the recording arrives regardless |

They are craft businesses, capped by their own labour, priced for the top of the market.
A one-man videography shop cannot profitably serve a thirty-minute birthday call and will
never try. That is not a technology gap that closes when they buy better software; it is a
cost-structure gap that only a marketplace closes.

**Diaspora-side funeral videographers.** UK Funeral Video Services streams African and
Caribbean funerals held *in Britain* to family back home.[^ukfvs] This is the mirror image of
our corridor and it is quietly the strongest validation in this document: families are
already paying, in both directions, for exactly this emotional job.

**Death-tech.** Safiri Salama in Kenya handles diaspora-funded funeral logistics and
transparency.[^safiri] Adjacent rather than competing, and a plausible partner or acquirer.

**Church streaming.** Many African churches already stream services that the diaspora
watches every Sunday. The behaviour — watching home from abroad, on a bad connection — is
established. Nobody has extended it from the congregation to the family.

### Who is not a competitor, and why it matters

**WhatsApp, Messenger, Google Meet.** They are the substrate, not the rival. They solve one
of the four constraints from [document 01](./01-problem.md) and will never solve the other
three, because Meta is not going to dispatch a human being with a power bank to Makélékélé.
Any strategy premised on out-engineering WhatsApp at video calling is lost before it starts.
Elongo's relationship to WhatsApp is parasitic in the useful sense: bookings, invitations and
viewing links all travel through it.

**Remittance companies.** Wise, Taptap Send, WorldRemit, Remitly and Sendwave own the
payment relationship but have no field operations and no claim on the emotional occasion.
They become relevant in phase 3, and the analysis there is that it is far easier for a
company that owns the wedding to add payments than for a company that owns payments to
acquire the wedding.

### The real substitute: doing it yourself

The honest competitor is a family that buys a €90 Android, a €20 power bank and a data
bundle, ships it home, and asks a cousin to be the cameraman. Take it seriously:

- **It costs about €130 once**, versus €39 per event. For a family with many events, DIY is
  cheaper on paper.
- **It fails on constraint four.** The cousin still has to stand there holding it. That is
  the constraint that most often ends the call, and hardware does not fix it.
- **It fails on entropy.** A spare phone in a Congolese household is absorbed into household
  use within weeks. This is the correct decision for the household and a total loss for the
  sender. Everyone who has tried this knows it.
- **It fails on the day.** The kit is at the aunt's house across town, uncharged, when the
  event starts.

The right response is not to argue. It is to price the **Appel** tier low enough (€15) that
trying Elongo once is cheaper than shipping a phone, and to let the experience make the
argument. It is also why phase 3 of [document 07](./07-roadmap-and-validation.md) eventually
*does* put a device in the home — but a solar-charged, single-purpose, remotely-managed one
that cannot be absorbed into household use, which is a different product from a spare phone.

## Why the beachhead is Congo-Brazzaville

Not because it is the biggest — it plainly is not. Because it is the best place to *learn*:

1. **Founder distribution.** This company will live or die on trust inside diaspora WhatsApp
   groups. That access cannot be bought, and it is only available to someone already inside
   the community.
2. **Nobody is there.** The whole competitive cluster is anglophone. Francophone Central
   Africa — Congo, DRC, Gabon, Cameroon, Chad, CAR — has no structured player at all.
3. **Hard mode is the right mode.** Congo has weak power (51% access), expensive data and
   patchy coverage. A product that works there works anywhere on the continent. Building
   first in Nairobi would produce a system that collapses the moment it moves.
4. **Adjacency is enormous.** DRC sits across the river with roughly 100 million people, a
   diaspora ten times the size, and the same language, the same culture, the same ceremonies
   and the same constraints. Brazzaville and Kinshasa are fifteen minutes apart by boat.
   Winning Brazzaville is a rehearsal for a market twenty times larger, using the same
   playbook, the same Lingala and the same operator training.

## The moat

Ranked by how hard each is to copy.

1. **Trust at funerals.** The slowest to build and the most valuable. A family that you
   served well during a death will use you for everything else for a decade and tell every
   relative they have. It cannot be bought, advertised into existence, or attacked by a
   better-funded competitor.
2. **Operator network density.** Two hundred vetted, trained, rated operators across
   Brazzaville, Pointe-Noire, Dolisie and Ouesso means you can serve an event on three hours'
   notice. That is a genuine cold-start problem for anyone following, and it compounds:
   density improves response time, which improves reviews, which improves bookings, which
   funds density.
3. **The family graph.** Who belongs to which family, who pays, who watches, whose events
   recur. This dataset is what makes the payments product work in phase 3 and it accrues
   passively from ordinary operation. A remittance company would have to buy it; it cannot.
4. **The resilience stack.** The engineering in [document 06](./06-technical-architecture.md)
   is genuinely hard — nine to twelve months of specialised work — and it is invisible right
   up until the moment it is the only reason the funeral got through. Copyable eventually,
   but not quickly, and not by a videography shop.

Note that the weakest moat is the technology and the strongest is the reputation. That
ordering should drive where the founder spends their own time: on operators and families,
not in the codebase.
